# T Cells

## 1. What a T Cell Recognizes

A **T cell** is an adaptive lymphocyte whose receptor recognizes antigen only when the antigen is displayed as peptide bound to MHC. This is called **MHC restriction**.

| T-cell type | Coreceptor | MHC recognized | Main role |
|-------------|------------|----------------|-----------|
| CD8 T cell | CD8 | MHC class I | Kill infected or abnormal host cells |
| CD4 T cell | CD4 | MHC class II | Help macrophages, B cells, and other immune cells |

This design makes T cells specialists for cell-associated information. A T cell asks: "What peptide is this host cell displaying, and in what context?"

That is different from an antibody. An antibody can bind the outside of a viral particle. A CD8 T cell detects that a host cell is producing viral proteins internally because fragments of those proteins appear on MHC I.

The molecular machinery behind a single recognition event:

- **The TCR** is a two-chain (αβ) receptor that contacts *both* the protruding residues of the bound peptide and the walls of the MHC groove. It is generated by random V(D)J recombination, which is the source of T-cell diversity.
- **The TCR has no signaling tail of its own.** It is packaged with the **CD3** complex (the CD3γ, δ, ε chains plus a ζ dimer), whose **ITAM** motifs are phosphorylated to carry the signal inward.
- **The coreceptor (CD8 or CD4)** clamps onto an invariant part of the MHC molecule — CD8 to the MHC I α3 domain, CD4 to the MHC II β2 domain — and brings the kinase **Lck** to the complex, sharply lowering the activation threshold. This physical coupling is why CD8 cells are "restricted" to MHC I and CD4 cells to MHC II.

## 2. Peptide-MHC and TCR Binding

Section 1 named the parts — the αβ TCR, the CD3 signaling module, and the CD8 or CD4 coreceptor. This section zooms into the event itself: *how* those parts read a peptide-MHC (**pMHC**) surface, why the bond is deliberately weak, and how a fleeting contact becomes a decision. Almost everything later in this chapter — the thymic selection thresholds, the three-signal interlock, cytotoxic targeting — is downstream of this single binding event.

**The TCR reads a composite surface with six loops.** The business end of the TCR is its pair of variable domains, Vα and Vβ. Each contributes three **complementarity-determining region (CDR)** loops — six in all — and they divide the labor between the fixed MHC frame and the variable peptide:

- **CDR1 and CDR2 are germline-encoded**, fixed by whichever V gene segment the cell rearranged. They sit mostly over the two α-helices that form the *walls* of the MHC groove. Because these loops are inherited rather than junctionally scrambled, TCRs carry a built-in tendency to bind MHC at all — a likely structural basis for both MHC restriction and alloreactivity (below).
- **CDR3α and CDR3β are hypervariable**, assembled at the V(D)J junctions where diversity is greatest. They fall over the *center* of the groove and directly read the few peptide side chains that bulge upward out of it.

The TCR docks in a conserved, roughly **diagonal** orientation across the pMHC — not square-on — so the germline loops reliably find the MHC helices while the two CDR3 loops meet over the middle of the peptide. The receptor thus judges a **composite surface**: a fixed MHC "frame" plus a variable peptide "message."

```text
   top-down view: the TCR footprint on peptide-MHC

        MHC helix  (one groove wall)
   =============================================
     \   CDR1a/CDR2a          CDR1b/CDR2b   /     germline loops -> MHC walls
      \            CDR3a  CDR3b            /
       \          [ p e p t i d e ]       /       CDR3 loops -> peptide center
      /                                    \
   =============================================
        MHC helix  (other groove wall)

   anchor residues point DOWN into MHC pockets (ch. 04) - hidden from the TCR
   a few central residues point UP - these are what CDR3 actually reads
```

The **anchor residues** that bury into the MHC pockets (chapter 4, section 3) are largely invisible to the TCR — they are the peptide's grip on the shelf. What the TCR grades is the handful of upward-pointing residues and the MHC surface framing them.

Is CDR recognition like "double locks"? Close, but not literally. It is better to think of it as a **two-part barcode reader**:

- **CDR1/CDR2** check the MHC frame.
- **CDR3** checks the peptide message sitting in that frame.

Both surfaces matter because the TCR recognizes one combined object, **peptide-MHC**. This prevents a T cell from reacting to free peptide alone and explains why the same peptide on the wrong MHC can look different to the TCR.

**The bond is weak, fast, and tunable — on purpose.** Antibodies bind their targets tightly, with dissociation constants in the nanomolar-to-picomolar range. The TCR-pMHC bond is, by contrast, strikingly loose and short-lived:

| Property | Antibody-antigen | TCR-pMHC |
|----------|------------------|----------|
| Affinity (Kd) | nM to pM (tight) | roughly 1-100 µM (weak) |
| Bond half-life | minutes to hours | often seconds or less |
| What is read | a surface epitope | peptide *plus* self-MHC frame |
| Effect of binding | neutralize, opsonize, fix complement | *trigger a signal*, then let go |

That looseness looks like a defect until you see what it buys:

- **Serial engagement.** Because each bond is brief, one pMHC complex can engage and trigger *many* TCRs in turn. A target displaying only a few hundred copies of the right peptide can still activate the T cell — huge sensitivity from scarce antigen.
- **Kinetic proofreading.** Converting a bond into a signal takes a *timed chain* of phosphorylation steps (below). A bond that breaks too soon resets the chain before it finishes, so only pMHC that stays bound long enough — an agonist peptide — drives the ladder to completion. This turns small differences in **bond lifetime (off-rate)** into large, switch-like differences in signaling, which is why dwell time predicts activation better than raw affinity does.
- **Coreceptor help and catch bonds.** CD8 or CD4 binds an invariant MHC domain, stabilizing the complex while delivering the kinase Lck. And the bond is **mechanically tuned**: as the migrating T cell tugs on the contact, agonist bonds can *lengthen* under force (a **catch bond**) while weak self-bonds slip apart. The TCR therefore behaves partly as a **mechanosensor**, using force itself to discriminate.

A longer-lived TCR-pMHC bond usually means this peptide-MHC is a better **agonist** for that TCR. It does **not** automatically mean "kill this cell" in every context. In a mature CD8 effector cell, a good match on MHC I can trigger killing. In a naive T cell, the same bond is only **Signal 1**; the cell still needs costimulation and cytokine context before it expands. In the thymus, strong binding to self peptide-MHC is interpreted as danger and causes deletion rather than immunity.

**From bond to signal: the handoff.** The TCR has no enzymatic tail, so recognition and signaling are physically coupled but chemically separate. The peptide is **not handed over** to CD3. Instead, the TCR binds peptide-MHC outside the cell, and the CD3/co-receptor machinery attached to the same membrane complex converts that binding event into phosphorylation inside the T cell.

| Part | What it binds | What it does |
|------|---------------|--------------|
| **TCR alpha-beta** | Peptide-MHC composite surface | Recognition head; decides specificity, but has almost no intracellular signaling capacity |
| **CD3 gamma/epsilon, delta/epsilon + zeta-zeta** | Does not bind peptide-MHC directly; is assembled with the TCR | Signal-transduction module; cytoplasmic **ITAMs** become phosphorylated and recruit ZAP-70 |
| **CD4 or CD8** | Invariant MHC domains: CD4 binds MHC II; CD8 binds MHC I | Co-receptor; stabilizes the correct class interaction and brings **Lck** next to CD3 ITAMs |
| **Lck** | Associated with CD4/CD8 cytoplasmic tails | Src-family tyrosine kinase; adds phosphate groups to CD3/zeta ITAMs and helps activate ZAP-70 |

```text
peptide-MHC engages TCR (+ CD8/CD4 coreceptor)
        |
        v
coreceptor-delivered LCK phosphorylates ITAMs on CD3 (gamma/delta/epsilon) and the zeta-zeta dimer
        |
        v
phosphorylated ITAMs recruit and activate ZAP-70
        |
        v
ZAP-70 phosphorylates the LAT scaffold -> a signalosome assembles
        |
        v
three arms fire:  Ca2+/calcineurin -> NFAT
                  DAG/Ras         -> MAPK
                  PKC-theta       -> NF-kB
        |
        v
transcriptional program (integrated with the CD28 signal) -> IL-2, proliferation, differentiation
```

In more detail:

```text
1. Dock
      TCR binds peptide-MHC; CD4 or CD8 binds the invariant MHC domain beside it.

2. Place Lck
      The co-receptor brings Lck into the same microcluster as CD3 and the zeta chain.

3. Phosphorylate ITAMs
      Lck phosphorylates tyrosines in CD3/zeta ITAMs, creating docking sites.

4. Recruit ZAP-70
      ZAP-70 binds phosphorylated ITAMs through SH2 domains and is activated by Lck.

5. Build LAT/SLP-76 signalosome
      ZAP-70 phosphorylates scaffold proteins that assemble PLC-gamma1,
      Ras/MAPK, and other signaling enzymes.

6. Turn on genes
      NFAT, AP-1, and NF-kappaB enter the nucleus; with CD28 costimulation
      they support IL-2 and proliferation.
```

Kinetic proofreading lives in the first two steps: the ITAM-to-ZAP-70 relay is the timer that a bond must outlast to matter. Weakness is therefore **not** needed to "pass the peptide to CD3"; CD3 never takes the peptide. Weakness is needed for fast scanning, serial engagement, and timed discrimination.

**Specificity versus cross-reactivity.** A person carries on the order of 10^7 to 10^8 distinct TCRs, but the space of possible 9-residue peptides is astronomically larger (20^9 is roughly half a trillion). The repertoire cannot hold a private receptor for every peptide, so **each TCR must be cross-reactive**: by keying on a few peptide residues plus the MHC frame, one TCR can recognize on the order of a million different peptides. Cross-reactivity is not sloppiness — it is how a finite repertoire covers a near-infinite antigenic world. Thymic negative selection (section 3) keeps it bounded by deleting clones that bind *self* pMHC too well.

That same germline affinity for MHC makes TCRs **alloreactive**: an unusually large fraction of T cells — on the order of 1 to 10 percent — respond directly to a *foreign* MHC allele presenting ordinary peptides, far above the frequency for any single conventional antigen. This is the immunological engine of **transplant rejection**, the very phenomenon that gave the "histocompatibility" complex its name (chapter 4).

## 3. T Cells Are Made Dangerous, Then Screened

T cells originate from bone-marrow progenitors but mature in the **thymus**. Their TCR genes are generated by V(D)J recombination (see [chapter 12](12-vdj-recombination-germinal-centers.md)). There they undergo selection.

```text
developing thymocyte
        |
        v
positive selection
   survives if TCR can recognize self MHC weakly enough to be useful
        |
        v
negative selection
   dies or diverts if TCR binds self peptide-MHC too strongly
        |
        v
mature naive T cell
```

Positive selection solves the "can this receptor work with this body's MHC?" problem. Negative selection reduces dangerous self-reactivity. The screening is not perfect, so peripheral tolerance mechanisms are also needed later.

Where and how each test happens:

- **Positive selection** occurs in the thymic cortex, where cortical epithelial cells present self peptide-MHC. A thymocyte that cannot bind self-MHC at all dies "by neglect"; one that binds *weakly* survives. The same step fixes lineage: reading MHC I commits the cell to the CD8 lineage, reading MHC II commits it to CD4.
- **Negative selection** occurs in the thymic medulla, where medullary epithelial cells and dendritic cells present a broad sample of self. A thymocyte whose TCR binds self peptide-MHC *too strongly* is deleted (clonal deletion) or diverted into the regulatory-T-cell lineage. This is **central tolerance**.
- **AIRE solves the tissue-antigen blind spot.** Negative selection can only delete clones against self-proteins the thymus actually displays. The transcription factor **AIRE (autoimmune regulator)** lets medullary thymic epithelial cells ectopically express thousands of tissue-restricted genes — insulin, thyroid proteins, retinal antigens — so that thymocytes reactive to peripheral organs are removed centrally. Loss-of-function AIRE mutations cause the multi-organ autoimmune syndrome **APECED**, direct evidence that central tolerance fails without it.

The randomness of TCR generation is both the power and the danger of T cells. The system can generate receptors for pathogens it has never seen, but it must constantly police receptors that could attack self.

### What If the Thymus Shrinks, Is Absent, or Is Removed Early?

The thymus does not simply become useless after childhood. It **involutes** with age — functional thymic epithelial tissue is progressively replaced by fat and stromal tissue — so the output of brand-new naive T cells falls. Adults maintain many T cells through long cell lifespan, memory pools, and peripheral homeostatic proliferation, but those routes preserve or expand existing clones; they do not generate the same breadth of new TCR diversity that a young thymus can.

| Situation | Expected immune consequence | Autoimmunity risk? |
|-----------|-----------------------------|--------------------|
| Normal aging thymus | Lower naive T-cell output, more reliance on existing naive/memory clones, narrower new-response capacity | Can contribute to immune dysregulation with age, but is not by itself a single-cause autoimmune disease |
| Partial thymic hypoplasia, as in many 22q11.2 / DiGeorge cases | Variable T-cell lymphopenia; many patients have partial, not absent, T-cell production | Yes, risk is increased because central tolerance and lymphocyte homeostasis can be abnormal |
| Complete congenital athymia | Severe T-cell immunodeficiency unless treated with specialized immune reconstitution such as thymus tissue implantation or transplant approaches | Immune deficiency dominates; defective selection can also permit immune dysregulation |
| Thymectomy in infancy or early childhood | Studies report long-term reduction in naive T cells and TCR diversity, especially when removal occurs before the peripheral T-cell pool is mature | Not inevitable, but some cohorts show increased immune dysregulation or autoimmune associations |

The age distinction matters. Losing thymic function in an adult is usually less catastrophic than being born without a thymus, because the adult already has a stocked peripheral T-cell compartment. Losing it very early is more serious because the immune system has not yet built enough diverse, self-tolerant T cells.

## 4. Naive T-Cell Activation

A naive T cell usually needs three kinds of information from an antigen-presenting cell.

| Signal | Meaning | Consequence if missing |
|--------|---------|------------------------|
| TCR binds peptide-MHC | Specific antigen recognition | No activation |
| Costimulation | APC has sensed danger | Anergy, deletion, or weak response |
| Cytokines | Type of threat and tissue context | Wrong or incomplete differentiation |

The three signals map onto specific molecules, and requiring all three is a deliberate safety interlock — antigen alone, without evidence of danger, leads not to activation but to anergy or deletion:

- **Signal 1 (antigen):** the TCR, with its CD4 or CD8 coreceptor, reads peptide-MHC. Specificity, but not permission.
- **Signal 2 (costimulation):** **CD28** on the T cell engages **B7 (CD80/CD86)** on the APC. B7 is only displayed after the APC's pattern-recognition receptors have fired, so Signal 2 certifies that the antigen was seen in a genuinely dangerous context. Without it, Signal 1 induces anergy rather than activation.
- **Signal 3 (cytokines):** the cytokine milieu (for example IL-12, IL-4, IL-6 plus TGF-β, or type I interferon) tells the cell what *kind* of threat it is facing and steers which effector or helper program it adopts.

Once activated, a T cell produces IL-2 and other signals, proliferates, and differentiates into effector cells. This is clonal expansion — the autocrine **IL-2** loop (the T cell makes IL-2 and upregulates its own high-affinity IL-2 receptor) is what drives the proliferative burst.

```text
one rare naive T cell
        |
        v
peptide-MHC + costimulation + cytokine context
        |
        v
many effector T cells with same antigen specificity
        |
        v
effector phase in infected tissue or lymphoid organ
```

The first response takes days because the matching clone must be found, activated, and expanded. A memory response is faster because the matching population is already larger and functionally primed.

## 5. CD8 T Cells: Killing Infected Cells

A **CD8 cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)** kills host cells that display matching peptide on MHC I. This is essential for viruses and other cytosolic pathogens.

The CTL killing sequence is focused:

```text
CTL binds target peptide-MHC I
        |
        v
immunological synapse forms
        |
        v
CTL releases cytotoxic granules toward target
        |
        v
perforin helps deliver granzymes
        |
        v
target cell undergoes apoptosis
        |
        v
CTL detaches and can kill another target
```

Two molecular routes deliver the kill, both ending in *controlled apoptosis* rather than messy rupture:

- **Perforin / granzyme route.** After the **immunological synapse** forms (an organized LFA-1/ICAM-1 adhesion ring that focuses the hit onto one cell), the CTL's microtubule organizing center reorients and polarizes its pre-formed lytic granules toward the synapse. **Perforin** permeabilizes the target membrane and helps deliver **granzyme B**, a protease that activates the caspase cascade from inside. Because release is confined to the sealed synapse, neighboring healthy cells are spared.
- **Fas–FasL route.** **FasL** on the CTL engages the death receptor **Fas (CD95)** on the target, triggering caspase-8 directly — a granule-independent path to the same apoptotic end.

Killing an infected cell by apoptosis packages its contents (including still-replicating virus) into membrane-bound fragments that phagocytes clear, sacrificing one host cell to stop pathogen production and limit spread. A single CTL detaches after each hit, reloads its granules, and moves on — **serial killing** of many targets.

## 6. CD4 T Cells: Helper Logic

**CD4 T cells** specialize into subsets that coordinate different immune strategies.

| Subset | Typical driver | Main outputs | Main use |
|--------|----------------|--------------|----------|
| TH1 | IL-12, intracellular microbes | IFN-gamma | Activate macrophages, support cell-mediated immunity |
| TH2 | IL-4, helminth-associated settings | IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 | Eosinophils, IgE, barrier responses |
| TH17 | IL-6, IL-23, TGF-beta contexts | IL-17, IL-22 | Neutrophil recruitment, barrier defense |
| T follicular helper | B-cell follicles and germinal centers | CD40L, IL-21 and other help | B-cell class switching and affinity maturation |
| Regulatory T cell | Tolerance-promoting contexts | IL-10, TGF-beta, suppressive mechanisms | Restrain immune responses |

Each subset is locked in by a **master transcription factor** switched on by the polarizing cytokine of Signal 3, which then enforces the signature cytokine output:

- **TH1** — master TF **T-bet**, signature output **IFN-γ**; driven by IL-12.
- **TH2** — master TF **GATA3**, signature outputs **IL-4, IL-5, IL-13**; driven by IL-4.
- **TH17** — master TF **RORγt**, signature outputs **IL-17, IL-22**; driven by IL-6/IL-23/TGF-β.
- **Tfh** — master TF **Bcl-6**, signature help **CD40L, IL-21**; arises in B-cell follicles.
- **Treg** — master TF **FOXP3**, signature outputs **IL-10, TGF-β**; arises in tolerogenic contexts.

These subsets are not rigid boxes. Real T-cell states are plastic and tissue-shaped. Still, the subset model is useful because it explains why "T-cell activation" can lead to very different outcomes: macrophage activation, antibody maturation, neutrophil recruitment, parasite-associated responses, or suppression.

## 7. T Cells and Macrophages: The TH1 Loop

Macrophages can ingest intracellular microbes but fail to kill them completely. TH1 cells provide the activation signal that pushes macrophages into a more microbicidal state.

```text
macrophage contains intracellular microbe
        |
        v
presents microbial peptide on MHC II
        |
        v
TH1 cell recognizes peptide-MHC II
        |
        v
TH1 cell provides IFN-gamma and CD40L
        |
        v
macrophage increases killing machinery
```

The two TH1 outputs act on distinct macrophage receptors: **IFN-γ** binds the IFN-γ receptor to license enhanced microbicidal programs, while **CD40L** on the T cell engages **CD40** on the macrophage to reinforce activation. The licensed macrophage then ramps up reactive oxygen species (via NADPH oxidase), nitric oxide (via iNOS), phagolysosome maturation, and more MHC II and B7 — a self-reinforcing handshake.

This loop explains granulomatous inflammation: when microbes are hard to eradicate, activated macrophages and T cells can build organized structures to contain them. Containment helps the host, but chronic containment can damage tissue.

## 8. T Cells and B Cells: The Germinal-Center Loop

T follicular helper cells are essential for high-quality antibody responses to many protein antigens.

A common confusion is to compress two helper-T-cell loops into one. A CD4 helper T cell can recognize peptide-MHC II on a **macrophage** and activate that macrophage; that is mainly the TH1 loop in section 7. For B-cell activation, the key APC is the **B cell itself**: the B cell must bind native antigen with its BCR, internalize it, and present peptide on MHC II to a Tfh cell.

The helper T cell does not carry antigen from the macrophage to the B cell. It delivers **permission signals** — especially CD40L and cytokines — to a B cell that has independently captured a linked antigen.

```text
usually first: dendritic cell primes naive CD4 T cell
        |
        v
CD4 T cell differentiates toward Tfh
        |
        v
B cell captures native antigen by BCR
        |
        v
B cell presents peptide on MHC II
        |
        v
Tfh cell recognizes peptide-MHC II
        |
        v
CD40L and cytokines drive B-cell proliferation
        |
        v
class switching, affinity maturation, plasma cells, memory B cells
```

This is **linked recognition**. The BCR may bind a surface shape on a virus coat, while the Tfh cell may recognize a peptide from a different protein inside the same virus particle. That is allowed because the B cell captured the whole linked object and processed it. What is not allowed is unrelated help: a Tfh cell specific for one antigen should not fully activate a B cell that captured an unrelated self molecule.

Without T-cell help, many antibody responses remain lower affinity, less durable, or restricted in class. This is why defects in CD40L-CD40 signaling can impair class-switched antibody production.

## 9. Regulatory T Cells Keep the System From Overshooting

A **regulatory T cell (Treg)** restrains immune activation. Tregs are central to peripheral tolerance. They can suppress other T cells, affect dendritic-cell costimulation, produce anti-inflammatory cytokines, and help keep responses proportional.

Tregs are defined by the master transcription factor **FOXP3**, and their suppressive toolkit includes several distinct mechanisms: sequestering the growth factor IL-2, stripping the costimulatory ligand B7 from APCs via **CTLA-4**, dampening dendritic-cell costimulation, and secreting the anti-inflammatory cytokines **IL-10 and TGF-β**.

This is not optional. Every infection creates collateral damage risk. Every receptor-generation process creates self-reactivity risk. Tregs are one of the mechanisms that keeps immune recognition from becoming autoimmune disease — loss-of-function **FOXP3** mutations cause the fatal multi-organ autoimmunity of **IPEX**, showing how essential this brake is.

## Summary

T cells inspect peptide-MHC displays rather than free antigen, through a deliberately weak, short-lived TCR bond whose duration — read out by kinetic proofreading — decides whether a signal fires. CD8 T cells kill infected or abnormal cells presenting matching peptides on MHC I. CD4 T cells coordinate immune strategy through helper subsets: macrophage activation, B-cell help, neutrophil recruitment, parasite-associated responses, and suppression. T cells are powerful because they combine clonal specificity with cellular effector functions; they are dangerous enough that thymic and peripheral tolerance are essential. How this biology is turned into medicine — checkpoint blockade of exhausted cells, CAR-T engineering, and bispecific T-cell engagers — is the subject of [chapter 7](07-t-cell-immunotherapy.md).
