⏱️ Response Timeline

The immune system is easier to understand as a sequence than as a cell list. Different mechanisms dominate at different times — and the order in which they fire is itself a design feature.

Core idea A defense is not a single weapon but a scheduled cascade. Fast, broad, pre-built innate mechanisms buy time in minutes to hours; slow, narrow, newly built adaptive mechanisms arrive in days and leave behind memory. The system escalates deliberately because its strongest weapons — cytotoxic killing and high-titer antibody — cause collateral damage if fired before the threat is confirmed.
1Why timelines matter

The same infection looks like a different disease depending on when you sample it. Early symptoms are mostly innate inflammation; later improvement often coincides with adaptive control. Reading the immune system as a clock — not a roster of cells — explains why both a fast, generic response and a slow, specific one are necessary.

The canonical ordering, from the source chapter:

minutes barriers, complement, resident macrophages, mast cells hours inflammation, cytokines, chemokines, neutrophil recruitment, NK activation days dendritic-cell migration, naive T-cell activation, B-cell activation, clonal expansion week and beyond antibodies improve, effector T cells peak, memory forms, inflammation resolves

The exact timing varies by tissue, dose, pathogen, prior immunity, age, and host condition. The sequence is still the durable lesson: pre-built defenses act instantly because no new genes or clones are needed, while adaptive defenses lag because the rare lymphocyte clone that matches the antigen must first be found and then multiplied roughly a thousandfold.

Two clocks, two reasons. Innate cells carry germline-encoded receptors (PRRs such as Toll-like receptors and NLRs) that are ready at birth, so recognition is immediate. Adaptive cells carry somatically rearranged receptors (BCR/TCR) unique to each clone, so the matching clone is vanishingly rare until expansion. Speed and specificity trade off — which is exactly why the body keeps both. See recognition (chapter 02) and antigen presentation (chapter 04).

2Scenario A — a bacterial splinter wound

Bacteria enter the skin through a splinter. This is the classic extracellular bacterial problem: the pathogen lives outside cells, so phagocytes, complement, and antibody are the relevant tools.

Minutes to hours — containment

Damaged keratinocytes release DAMPs (damage-associated molecular patterns: ATP, uric acid, HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA). Resident macrophages and tissue mast cells sense both these danger signals and bacterial PAMPs (LPS via TLR4, lipopeptides via TLR2). In parallel, the alternative and lectin complement pathways deposit C3b directly on bacterial surfaces within seconds, no antibody required.

skin breach | v resident macrophage + mast cell detect PAMPs/DAMPs | +--> phagocytosis (FcγR / CR3 grip opsonized bacteria) +--> TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 (NF-κB + inflammasome) +--> CXCL8 / chemokines (recruitment gradient) +--> histamine (mast cell) --> vasodilation, leak | v neutrophils roll, arrest on activated endothelium, extravasate | v complement C3a / C5a sharpen the gradient; C5b-9 punches membrane pores

The first goal is purely containment: keep microbes local, flood the site with phagocytes, and stop bloodstream spread. The four cardinal signs of inflammation — redness, heat, swelling, pain (rubor, calor, tumor, dolor) — are the visible side effects of vasodilation, plasma-protein leak, and nerve sensitization by bradykinin and prostaglandins.

Hours to day 1 — the neutrophil surge

Neutrophils are the most numerous early responders. They phagocytose opsonized bacteria, release antimicrobial granules, and extrude NETs (neutrophil extracellular traps — webs of chromatin studded with enzymes) to snare bacteria. Pus is largely spent neutrophils. They are short-lived by design: a cell that floods tissue with oxidants and proteases must self-destruct quickly.

What is a neutrophil? A neutrophil is a short-lived myeloid granulocyte made in the bone marrow and released into blood. It is not a lymphocyte. It has a segmented multi-lobed nucleus and many granules, so it is also called a polymorphonuclear leukocyte or PMN.

Days — adaptive reinforcement

Antigen-loaded dendritic cells migrate to the draining lymph node. Naive T cells scan peptide–MHC displays; the rare matching clone is licensed and expands. B cells whose BCRs bind bacterial antigen internalize it, present it on MHC II, and receive help from follicular helper T cells. For an extracellular pathogen, the decisive output is antibody: IgM first, then class-switched IgG, which opsonizes bacteria (FcγR-mediated phagocytosis), activates the classical complement pathway, and neutralizes toxins.

Resolution

Once bacteria are cleared, neutrophils die by apoptosis and macrophages engulf them — efferocytosis — which is actively anti-inflammatory and switches macrophages toward a repair (M2-like) program. Lipid mediators shift from pro-inflammatory prostaglandins/leukotrienes to specialized pro-resolving mediators (resolvins, lipoxins, protectins). Inflammatory signals fall, repair signals rise, and a fraction of the responding B and T cells persist as memory. See macrophages (chapter 03) and antibodies (chapter 05).

3Scenario B — a viral respiratory infection

Viruses replicate inside host cells. Antibody can neutralize free virions between cells, but once the genome is intracellular, antibody is blind to it — so the system needs an arm that inspects what each cell is making. That arm reads peptides on MHC I.

Early phase — the interferon firewall

Infected epithelial cells and dendritic cells sense viral nucleic acids: cytosolic double-stranded RNA via RIG-I/MDA5 and endosomal RNA via TLR3/7/8; foreign DNA via the cGAS–STING pathway. The shared output is type I interferon (IFN-α/β), which signals neighboring cells through JAK–STAT to switch on hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes — degrading viral RNA, blocking translation, and raising the alarm before the virus arrives.

virus enters epithelial cell | v viral RNA/DNA sensed (RIG-I/MDA5, TLR3/7/8, cGAS-STING) | +--> type I interferon (IFN-α/β) --> antiviral state in neighbors +--> inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1, IL-12, IL-18) | v NK cells kill stressed or MHC-low cells (IFN-α/β + IL-12/IL-18 raise NK cytotoxicity and IFN-γ output)

Type I interferon also activates NK cells. Many viruses downregulate MHC I to hide infected-cell peptides from CD8 T cells — but that very loss removes the inhibitory signal NK cells depend on, tripping the missing-self alarm. NK cells thus contain spread for the first few days, before virus-specific CD8 T cells are numerous. See NK cells (chapter 08).

Adaptive phase — three labors divided

In the lymph node, dendritic cells present viral peptides on both MHC I (via cross-presentation, so CD8 cells can be primed against a virus that never infected the DC) and MHC II. CD8 T cells expand into cytotoxic T lymphocytes; CD4 helper cells (Th1 and Tfh) license dendritic cells, sustain CD8 responses, and drive B-cell antibody production. The work splits cleanly by where the virus is:

free virus outside cells -> antibody neutralization (blocks receptor binding / entry) infected cells producing viral proteins -> MHC I display of viral peptides -> CD8 T-cell killing (perforin + granzyme; Fas–FasL) MHC-low / stressed infected cells -> NK-cell killing (missing-self + stress ligands)

The two cytotoxic routes are complementary, not redundant: CD8 T cells require MHC I to act, so viruses that hide MHC I escape them — and walk straight into the NK cells that punish MHC-I loss. Both kill via the same machinery (perforin pores let granzyme proteases enter and trigger apoptosis).

Does antibody automatically recruit NK cells?

Not always. Antibody can recruit several effector systems, and which one dominates depends on what the antibody is stuck to.

For antibody-coated microbes, neutrophils and macrophages are usually the cleaner fit because they can engulf the particle.

Memory

After clearance, memory B cells, long-lived plasma cells in the bone marrow, central- and effector-memory CD4 cells, and memory CD8 cells (including lung-resident memory) remain. On re-exposure, pre-existing mucosal antibody may block infection at the airway surface entirely, and memory T cells respond in hours rather than days. See memory (chapter 10).

T-cell memory is real, but it looks different from B-cell memory. A memory B response can leave antibody already floating in serum. A memory T cell does not secrete a soluble receptor. Instead, the body keeps more antigen-specific T cells than before, in a primed state: central memory T cells refill the response from lymph nodes, effector memory T cells patrol blood and tissues, and tissue-resident memory T cells stay at barrier sites such as lung, gut, and skin. Memory CD8 cells also survive by homeostatic cytokines such as IL-7 and IL-15 and can regain killing function faster than naive CD8 cells.

4Scenario C — vaccination

A vaccine tries to teach adaptive immunity without causing the full disease. It deliberately feeds the natural sequence two things — antigen plus innate context — so that memory forms while the pathogen burden stays at zero.

For a protein-antigen vaccine, the staged path mirrors a real infection — minus the pathogen:

antigen + adjuvant enter tissue | v dendritic cells capture antigen and sense adjuvant context (PRR firing) | v (migrate to draining lymph node, upregulate MHC + CD80/86) | naive T cells activate: signal 1 (peptide-MHC) + signal 2 (costim) + signal 3 (cytokine) | v B cells bind antigen, present to and receive Tfh help (CD40L + IL-21) | v germinal centers run somatic hypermutation --> higher-affinity antibody | v long-lived plasma cells + memory B / memory T cells persist

Success depends on far more than antigen identity. An antigen delivered with no adjuvant is often ignored or even tolerized, because without signal 2 (costimulation) and signal 3 (instructive cytokines) the dendritic cell never tells the T cell the antigen is dangerous. The adjuvant supplies the artificial "danger" that the absent pathogen would otherwise provide.

Why boosters work A second dose re-enters memory B cells into germinal centers, where another round of somatic hypermutation and selection raises antibody affinity and broadens the response. This is the deliberate use of the secondary response curve — faster onset, higher titer — covered in chapter 10.

Why innate peaks first

Macrophages, complement, and NK cells use germline-encoded recognition and are already on station. No clone-finding, no expansion — recognition equals action within minutes.

Why adaptive peaks late

The matching B or T clone starts as perhaps one cell in a million. It must be found, licensed, and expanded ~1000-fold over days before its effector output (antibody, cytotoxic killing) can dent the pathogen.

6Why the system does not fire the strongest weapon first

It would be catastrophic to activate every cytotoxic T cell and plasma cell at the first hint of inflammation. The strongest weapons are also the least discriminating in aggregate: cytotoxic killing destroys host cells, high antibody titers can form immune complexes, and a runaway cytokine response can itself kill the host (a cytokine storm). The system therefore escalates, spending the cheapest, safest, most reversible options first and committing to the expensive, dangerous ones only after the threat is confirmed and localized.

local broad response (cheap, fast, reversible — innate) | v evidence gathering & antigen transport (DC migration to lymph node) | v specific clonal expansion (commit only the matching clone) | v targeted effector response (antibody + cytotoxicity, where needed) | v contraction & memory (stand down; keep a record)

Each step is a checkpoint. T cells need three independent signals to commit; B cells need T-cell help to make high-affinity antibody; effector responses contract sharply once antigen is cleared. This staged escalation explains many clinical patterns: early symptoms reflect innate inflammation, later improvement coincides with adaptive control, and severe disease can arise from three distinct failures — overwhelming pathogen burden, immune-mediated tissue damage, or failure to resolve and stand down.

The escalation logic, in one line Fire the broadest, safest, fastest tool first; confirm the threat; then commit narrow, powerful, slow weapons only against a verified target — and tear them down the moment the threat is gone. Skipping the staging is how immunopathology happens.
Summary Immune responses unfold over time. Macrophages, complement, cytokines, neutrophils, and NK cells act early because their recognition is germline-encoded and pre-built. Dendritic cells then carry antigen and innate context to lymph nodes, where rare matching B and T clones are found and expanded over days before returning with precise effector functions. Vaccination works by deliberately feeding this sequence antigen plus innate context, so durable memory forms without full disease — and the whole cascade is staged, not simultaneous, precisely because the strongest weapons are the most dangerous to the host.
