🎯 T Cells

T cells do not read free antigen — they read the surfaces of host cells. By inspecting peptide displayed on MHC, a T cell asks one precise question: "What is this cell making inside, and should I act on it?"

Core idea A T cell is a peptide-MHC reader built for cell-associated information. Its randomly generated receptor is forged in the thymus to be useful but not self-reactive, then armed in the periphery only when an antigen-presenting cell delivers three signals at once. CD8 T cells turn that reading into controlled killing of infected cells; CD4 T cells turn it into licensing signals that steer macrophages, B cells, and the rest of the response.
1What a T cell recognizes

A T cell is an adaptive lymphocyte whose receptor (the TCR) recognizes antigen only when the antigen is displayed as a short peptide bound inside an MHC molecule on another cell's surface. This requirement is called MHC restriction — proven by Doherty and Zinkernagel, who showed a T cell only kills an infected target if they share the same MHC allele.

The TCR itself is a two-chain (αβ) receptor that contacts both the protruding residues of the peptide and the walls of the MHC groove. It carries no signaling tail of its own; the associated CD3 complex (with its ITAM motifs) transmits the signal inward. A coreceptor — CD8 or CD4 — clamps onto an invariant part of the MHC molecule and recruits the kinase Lck, sharply lowering the activation threshold.

Why this design matters. An antibody can grip the outside of a virus particle. But a CD8 T cell can tell that a host cell is internally producing viral protein, because fragments of that protein are displayed on its MHC I. T cells are therefore the specialists for cell-associated, internal information. See antigen presentation (chapter 04) for how peptides reach MHC I versus MHC II.
2Peptide-MHC and TCR binding

Section 1 named the parts — the αβ TCR, the CD3 signaling module, and the CD8/CD4 coreceptor. This section zooms into the event itself: how those parts read a peptide-MHC (pMHC) surface, why the bond is deliberately weak, and how a fleeting contact becomes a decision. Almost everything later in this chapter — thymic selection thresholds, the three-signal interlock, cytotoxic targeting — is downstream of this one binding event.

The TCR reads a composite surface with six loops

The business end of the TCR is its pair of variable domains, Vα and Vβ. Each contributes three complementarity-determining region (CDR) loops — six in all — split between the fixed MHC frame and the variable peptide.

CDR1 & CDR2 — germline → read the MHC

Fixed by whichever V gene segment rearranged. They sit over the two α-helices that form the walls of the MHC groove. Being inherited rather than junctionally scrambled, they give TCRs a built-in tendency to bind MHC at all — a structural basis for both MHC restriction and alloreactivity.

CDR3α & CDR3β — hypervariable → read the peptide

Assembled at the V(D)J junctions where diversity peaks. They fall over the center of the groove and directly read the few peptide side chains that bulge upward out of it.

The TCR docks in a conserved, roughly diagonal orientation across the pMHC — not square-on — so the germline loops reliably find the MHC helices while the two CDR3 loops meet over the middle of the peptide. It judges a composite surface: a fixed MHC "frame" plus a variable peptide "message."

The anchors are invisible to the TCR. The peptide's anchor residues bury into the MHC pockets (see antigen presentation, chapter 04) — they are its grip on the shelf. What the TCR grades is the handful of upward-pointing residues and the MHC surface framing them.
Is CDR recognition like double locks? Close, but not exactly. It is more like a two-part barcode reader: CDR1/CDR2 check the MHC frame, while CDR3 checks the peptide message sitting in that frame. Both surfaces matter because the TCR recognizes one combined object, peptide-MHC. This prevents a T cell from reacting to free peptide alone and explains why the same peptide on the wrong MHC can look different to the TCR.

A weak, fast, tunable bond — on purpose

Antibodies bind tightly (dissociation constants in the nanomolar-to-picomolar range). The TCR-pMHC bond is, by contrast, strikingly loose and short-lived.

That looseness looks like a defect until you see what it buys:

Because each bond is brief, one pMHC can trigger many TCRs in turn — a few hundred copies of the right peptide can activate the cell.
DISCRIMINATION

Signaling takes a timed chain of steps; a bond that breaks too soon resets it. Only long-enough (agonist) bonds finish the ladder — so dwell time, not affinity, predicts activation.
FORCE
Catch bonds
CD8/CD4 stabilize the complex and deliver Lck. Under the pull of a migrating cell, agonist bonds lengthen while self-bonds slip — the TCR as a mechanosensor.

A longer-lived TCR-pMHC bond usually means this peptide-MHC is a better agonist for that TCR. It does not automatically mean "kill this cell" in every context. In a mature CD8 effector cell, a good match on MHC I can trigger killing. In a naive T cell, the same bond is only Signal 1; the cell still needs costimulation and cytokine context before it expands. In the thymus, strong binding to self peptide-MHC is interpreted as danger and causes deletion rather than immunity.

From bond to signal: the handoff

The TCR has no enzymatic tail, so recognition and signaling are physically coupled but chemically separate. The peptide is not handed over to CD3. Instead, the TCR binds peptide-MHC outside the cell, and the CD3/co-receptor machinery attached to the same membrane complex converts that binding event into phosphorylation inside the T cell.

Why weakness is the point Kinetic proofreading lives in the ITAM → ZAP-70 relay: it is a timer the bond must outlast. A weak, fast bond lets the cell test thousands of pMHC per minute and commit only to the ones that dwell long enough — high sensitivity and high specificity from the same loose grip. Weakness is therefore not needed to "pass the peptide to CD3"; CD3 never takes the peptide. Weakness is needed for fast scanning, serial engagement, and timed discrimination.

Specificity versus cross-reactivity

A person carries on the order of 107–108 distinct TCRs, but the space of possible 9-residue peptides is astronomically larger (209 is roughly half a trillion). The repertoire cannot hold a private receptor for every peptide, so each TCR must be cross-reactive: by keying on a few peptide residues plus the MHC frame, one TCR can recognize on the order of a million different peptides. Cross-reactivity is not sloppiness — it is how a finite repertoire covers a near-infinite antigenic world; thymic negative selection (section 3) keeps it bounded by deleting clones that bind self pMHC too well.

Alloreactivity: why transplants are rejected The same germline affinity for MHC makes TCRs alloreactive — an unusually large fraction of T cells (roughly 1–10%) respond directly to a foreign MHC allele presenting ordinary peptides, far above the frequency for any single conventional antigen. That is the immunological engine of transplant rejection, the phenomenon that gave the "histocompatibility" complex its name. See chapter 04.
3T cells are made dangerous, then screened in the thymus

T cells originate from bone-marrow progenitors but mature in the thymus. Each developing thymocyte rearranges its TCR genes randomly (V(D)J recombination; see chapter 12), so the starting repertoire contains receptors for pathogens never seen — and, inevitably, receptors that bind self. The thymus runs two sequential tests to keep the useful ones and remove the dangerous ones.

AIRE solves the "tissue antigen" problem Negative selection can only delete a clone against self-proteins the thymus actually shows. The transcription factor AIRE (autoimmune regulator) lets medullary thymic epithelial cells ectopically express thousands of tissue-restricted genes — insulin, thyroid proteins, retinal antigens — so that thymocytes reactive to peripheral organs can be removed centrally. Loss of AIRE causes the multi-organ autoimmune syndrome APECED, proving central tolerance is incomplete without it. Because screening is never perfect, peripheral tolerance (anergy, Tregs, ignorance) is also required later. See the response timeline (chapter 09) and tolerance mechanisms in chapter 10.

What if the thymus shrinks, is absent, or is removed early?

The thymus does not simply become useless after childhood. It involutes with age — functional thymic epithelial tissue is progressively replaced by fat and stromal tissue — so the output of brand-new naive T cells falls. Adults maintain many T cells through long cell lifespan, memory pools, and peripheral homeostatic proliferation, but those routes preserve or expand existing clones; they do not generate the same breadth of new TCR diversity that a young thymus can.

The age distinction matters. Losing thymic function in an adult is usually less catastrophic than being born without a thymus, because the adult already has a stocked peripheral T-cell compartment. Losing it very early is more serious because the immune system has not yet built enough diverse, self-tolerant T cells.
4Naive T-cell activation: the three-signal model

A naive T cell is deliberately hard to switch on. It usually needs three independent pieces of information from an antigen-presenting cell — typically a dendritic cell that has migrated from infected tissue to a lymph node. Requiring all three is a safety interlock: antigen alone, without evidence of danger, leads not to activation but to anergy or deletion.

5CD8 cytotoxic T cells: killing infected cells

A CD8 cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) kills host cells that display a matching peptide on MHC I — the surveillance system for viruses and other cytosolic pathogens. Because almost every nucleated cell expresses MHC I and continuously samples its own cytosolic proteins, a virus that hijacks a cell to make viral protein cannot hide: fragments appear on MHC I and flag the cell for death. The lethal molecules are delivered into a tight synapse, so the target cell receives the hit while neighboring cells are spared. The CTL can detach and kill again.

Two design points are worth singling out:

Perforin / granzyme route

Pre-formed lytic granules deliver perforin (forms pores / aids delivery) and granzyme B (a protease that activates the caspase cascade). The granules are released only into the tightly sealed synapse, so neighboring healthy cells are spared.

Fas–FasL route

FasL on the CTL engages the death receptor Fas (CD95) on the target, triggering caspase-8 directly. A second, granule-independent path to the same end: controlled apoptosis.

Apoptosis, not rupture, is the point. Killing by apoptosis packages the infected cell's contents (including its still-replicating virus) into membrane-bound fragments cleared by phagocytes — limiting spread and inflammation. Sacrificing one host cell stops it from being a pathogen factory.
6CD4 helper T cells: one recognition event, many strategies

CD4 T cells rarely kill directly. Instead they read peptide-MHC II on professional APCs and then differentiate — guided by Signal 3 cytokines — into helper subsets, each running a master transcription factor and a signature cytokine output. The subset chosen determines whether "T-cell activation" means macrophage activation, antibody maturation, neutrophil recruitment, anti-parasite defense, or suppression.

The subset model is still valuable because it explains how a single recognition event can lead to opposite outcomes — from licensing a macrophage to kill, to suppressing the whole response.
7The TH1 loop: licensing macrophages to finish the kill

Some pathogens (notably mycobacteria) survive inside macrophage vesicles. The macrophage can ingest them but cannot complete the kill on its own — it needs a license from a TH1 cell. This is a reciprocal handshake: the macrophage shows the antigen, the TH1 cell verifies it and sends back two activating signals.

Containment protects the host, but chronic containment damages tissue — the same double-edged logic seen with macrophages. See the matching loop from the macrophage side in chapter 03.

8The Tfh loop: licensing B cells in the germinal center

T follicular helper (Tfh) cells are essential for high-quality antibody responses to most protein antigens. The handshake mirrors the macrophage loop, but the help drives antibody quality instead of microbicidal killing.

Split the helper loops. A CD4 helper T cell can recognize peptide-MHC II on a macrophage and activate that macrophage; that is mainly the TH1 loop above. For B-cell activation, the key APC is the B cell itself: it captures native antigen with its BCR, presents peptide-MHC II, and receives Tfh permission signals. The helper T cell does not transfer antigen from macrophage to B cell.

The B cell and T cell do not need to bind the same epitope, but their epitopes must come from the same captured antigenic object. That linked-recognition rule reduces accidental help to unrelated B cells.

This is linked recognition. The BCR may bind a surface shape on a virus coat, while the Tfh cell may recognize a peptide from a different protein inside the same virus particle. That is allowed because the B cell captured the whole linked object and processed it. What is not allowed is unrelated help: a Tfh cell specific for one antigen should not fully activate a B cell that captured an unrelated self molecule.

Without T-cell help, many antibody responses remain lower-affinity, less durable, or restricted in class. This is why defects in CD40L–CD40 signaling impair class-switched antibody production (hyper-IgM syndrome). See B cells & antibodies (chapter 05) and V(D)J & germinal centers (chapter 12).

9Regulatory T cells keep the system from overshooting

A regulatory T cell (Treg), defined by the master transcription factor FOXP3, actively restrains immune activation. Tregs are central to peripheral tolerance: they suppress other T cells, sequester IL-2, dampen dendritic-cell costimulation (e.g. via CTLA-4 stripping B7 from APCs), and secrete anti-inflammatory IL-10 and TGF-β to keep responses proportional.

This is not optional bookkeeping. Every infection carries collateral-damage risk, and every random receptor-generation event carries self-reactivity risk. Tregs are a key mechanism that keeps immune recognition from tipping into autoimmune disease — loss-of-function FOXP3 mutations cause the fatal multi-organ autoimmunity of IPEX. See tolerance and its failures in chapter 10.

Summary T cells inspect peptide-MHC displays rather than free antigen — through a deliberately weak, short-lived TCR bond whose dwell time, not its affinity, decides whether a signal fires (kinetic proofreading) — after a thymic apprenticeship (positive selection for MHC literacy, negative selection plus AIRE for self-tolerance) and a three-signal activation interlock. CD8 CTLs kill infected cells presenting matching peptide on MHC I, using the perforin/granzyme and Fas–FasL apoptosis routes inside a tightly focused synapse. CD4 helpers convert one recognition event into many strategies — TH1 licensing of macrophages, Tfh licensing of B cells, TH2/TH17 barrier programs, and Treg suppression. T cells are powerful because they fuse clonal specificity with cellular effector and licensing functions; they are dangerous enough that thymic and peripheral tolerance are not luxuries but requirements. How this biology becomes medicine — checkpoint blockade of exhausted cells, CAR-T engineering, and bispecific T-cell engagers — is the subject of chapter 07.
