🍽️ Macrophages

The "big eater" is really a tissue-resident decision cell: sentinel, phagocyte, cytokine broadcaster, antigen presenter, killer, and repair organizer — often all in one lifetime.

Core idea A macrophage's defining job is conversion: it turns a local physical event ("there are bacteria here") into both an immediate action (engulf and kill) and a tissue-wide signal ("blood vessels, plasma proteins, neutrophils, and lymphocytes should behave differently here").
1What a macrophage is — and where it comes from

A macrophage is a tissue-resident phagocytic immune cell. "Big eater" is accurate but incomplete: eating is one of at least six jobs. Macrophages also sense danger, secrete cytokines, present antigen, clear dead cells, and decide whether inflammation escalates or resolves.

Two developmental origins coexist, which matters for how tissues behave:

Monocyte-derived

Bone-marrow monocytes circulate, enter inflamed tissue, and differentiate locally. These dominate during active infection and injury — the reinforcements.

Embryonically seeded, self-renewing

Many resident populations are laid down before birth (often from yolk-sac/fetal-liver progenitors) and maintain themselves by local proliferation, largely independent of bone marrow — the standing garrison.

3Phagocytosis at the molecular level

The animation above shows the choreography; here is the machinery behind each beat. Phagocytosis is receptor-triggered — the macrophage does not engulf indiscriminately, it engulfs what its receptors grip.

STEP 1
Recognition
FcγR (binds antibody Fc), complement receptors CR1/CR3 (bind C3b/iC3b), scavenger receptors, and PRRs grip the target.
STEP 2
Signaling
FcγR ITAM motifs are phosphorylated; Rho-family GTPases (Rac, Cdc42) and the Arp2/3 complex drive actin polymerization.
STEP 3
Engulfment
Actin pushes the membrane into pseudopods that zipper around the particle and seal it into a phagosome.
STEP 4
Maturation
The phagosome swaps Rab5 → Rab7 markers and fuses with lysosomes; a V-ATPase pumps protons, dropping pH to about 4.5.
STEP 5
Killing
The NADPH oxidase (phox) "respiratory burst" makes reactive oxygen species; iNOS makes nitric oxide; proteases and defensins finish the job.
STEP 6
Display
Peptide fragments are loaded onto MHC II and shown to CD4 T cells — recognition becomes information.

Two recognition routes are worth singling out because they connect innate killing to the rest of immunity:

Tag on targetReceptorWhat it means
Antibody Fc regionFc receptor (FcγR)Adaptive immunity has marked this target
C3b / iC3bComplement receptor (CR1/CR3)Complement has marked this target
Opsonization changes behavior, not just labeling. A weakly recognized bacterium can become easy prey once coated with antibody or C3b. The tag (recognition) and the eating (effector action) are separable — a recurring immune design principle. See complement (chapter 02) and antibodies (chapter 05).

Why macrophages do not simply eat free antibody

Your concern is exactly the right safety question. Blood contains huge amounts of free IgG, and macrophages express Fc receptors, so why are they not constantly swallowing normal antibody? The answer is that phagocytosis needs surface clustering, not just one antibody molecule touching one receptor.

Free IgG is signal-poor; surface-coated IgG is signal-rich

Free antibody in plasma

single IgG ↔ one FcγR

Weak, transient contact. No stable receptor cluster, no actin cup, no particle-sized target to wrap.

Antibody-coated microbe

many Fc stems
fixed on surface → FcγR cluster
ITAM signal → actin zipper
phagosome

Many adjacent Fc receptors fire together, and the macrophage membrane can zipper around a real particle.

Large soluble immune complexes can be cleared by Fc and complement receptors, but single normal antibodies are not a phagocytosis target. The cell responds to geometry: clustered Fc on a surface.
Safety layerWhat it prevents
Low effective signaling from monomeric IgGA single floating antibody does not cross-link enough Fc receptors to trigger the engulfment program
Particle-size requirementPhagocytosis needs a surface the membrane can wrap around; soluble IgG has no such geometry
Host Fc handlingFcRn binds IgG inside acidic endosomes and rescues many molecules from degradation, recycling them back to blood and extending IgG half-life
Complement and Fc regulationHost cells carry complement regulators and inhibitory receptor tuning that prevent accidental escalation on self surfaces
Exception: immune-complex clearance If many antibodies bind the same soluble antigen, they can form large immune complexes. Those complexes may activate complement and be cleared by macrophages in the liver and spleen. That is normal cleanup when controlled, but excessive immune-complex deposition can drive disease. This is different from a macrophage eating ordinary free antibody one molecule at a time.

5The TH1 feedback loop

Some pathogens (e.g. mycobacteria) survive inside macrophage vesicles. The macrophage can ingest them but cannot finish the kill alone — it needs a license from a CD4 T cell.

macrophage engulfs intracellular bacterium | v presents bacterial peptide on MHC II | v TH1 cell recognizes peptide-MHC II | v TH1 delivers IFN-γ + CD40L signal | v macrophage ramps up microbicidal machinery (ROS, NO)

When the kill still can't be finished, activated macrophages and T cells wall the pathogen off inside a granuloma. Containment protects the host — but chronic containment damages tissue. See T cells (chapter 06).

6M1 / M2: a coordinate system, not two species

M1-like

Induced by: microbial products + IFN-γ.
Behaves: inflammatory, microbicidal, high IL-12.

M2-like

Induced by: IL-4, IL-13, repair environments.
Behaves: tissue repair, anti-parasite responses, remodeling.

Treat M1/M2 as axes, not boxes Real tissue macrophages live in mixed, dynamic states. A tumor-associated macrophage, an alveolar macrophage, and a wound-healing macrophage may share "M2-like" markers yet behave very differently.
7Macrophages must also end inflammation

A response that cannot stop becomes pathology. Macrophages resolve inflammation by clearing apoptotic neutrophils — efferocytosis — which is actively anti-inflammatory because it removes dying cells before they spill DAMPs.

infection controlled → neutrophils die by apoptosis → macrophages engulf the apoptotic cells (efferocytosis) → inflammatory signals fall, repair signals rise

This is why macrophages are double-edged in chronic disease: the same repair program, if it never terminates, drives fibrosis, granulomas, atherosclerotic plaques, and tumor-supportive niches. See chronic inflammation (chapter 10).

Summary Macrophages are resident tissue sentinels that detect danger, engulf and kill targets, broadcast inflammation, present antigen, receive T-cell help, clear dead cells, and coordinate repair. Their central role is conversion: turning local damage into both immediate effector action and the signals that steer the rest of the immune system.
