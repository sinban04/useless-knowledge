🚨 Recognition & Inflammation

How the body detects "something is wrong" before any antibody exists — pattern-recognition receptors, the complement cascade, and inflammation as a controlled tissue program that gates everything adaptive immunity does next.

Core idea Innate immunity does not wait to learn a pathogen. It reads conserved molecular patterns — microbial structures the body could never have evolved, and host molecules that should never be loose in tissue — through germline-encoded receptors and soluble cascades. Detection is instantly converted into two outputs: tag-and-kill (opsonization, the membrane attack complex) and broadcast (cytokines, chemokines, inflammation) that decides whether adaptive B and T cells are licensed to respond.
1Innate immunity starts before cells arrive

Innate immunity is the set of inherited defenses that operate without waiting for a new B-cell or T-cell clone to expand. It begins with physical and chemical barriers — the response is already in place before any infection occurs.

Once a microbe crosses a barrier, the immune system needs a fast way to decide whether tissue has been invaded. That decision is made by cells and soluble proteins that recognize patterns common to microbes or damaged host cells — not the identity of any one strain.

2Pattern recognition: PAMPs and DAMPs

A pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) is a conserved microbial feature that host cells can recognize. A damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) is a host-derived signal that appears when tissue is injured or cells die abnormally. The genius of using patterns is that the receptors are germline-encoded — fixed in the DNA — so the response is instant, but limited to molecular signatures that microbes cannot easily abandon.

Cells detect these patterns with pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs). A macrophage does not need to know the exact bacterial strain to respond to LPS; it only needs to recognize that LPS is a microbial surface pattern. This gives innate immunity speed — and a limitation: pathogens that hide conserved patterns, live inside host cells, or mimic host surfaces can evade broad detection.

The PRR families — one sensor set per compartment

PRRs are not one receptor but several structurally distinct families, each watching a different cellular compartment. The location of the sensor is itself a clue: nucleic acids in the cytosol almost always mean an intruder, because the cell's own DNA stays in the nucleus and its RNA is processed and capped.

The inflammasome is a two-signal device. Signal 1 (a TLR firing) primes the cell to transcribe pro-IL-1β. Signal 2 (a DAMP such as extracellular ATP or crystals sensed by NLRP3) assembles the inflammasome, which activates caspase-1. Caspase-1 cleaves pro-IL-1β into mature, secreted IL-1β and can trigger pyroptosis — an inflammatory cell death that dumps alarm signals. This is why DAMPs and PAMPs together produce a much louder response than either alone.

Most surface and endosomal TLRs converge on the adaptors MyD88 (and TRIF for TLR3/TLR4), which leads — through IRAK and TRAF6 kinases — to activation of the master inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB. The cytosolic nucleic-acid sensors (RLRs, cGAS–STING) route instead toward type I interferon (IFN-α/β) genes, the antiviral program.

3Complement: soluble pattern recognition in blood

The complement system is a cascade of about thirty plasma proteins, made mostly by the liver and circulating as inactive precursors. When triggered, each component cleaves the next, so a small initiating event amplifies into a large surface coating — the same proteolytic-amplification logic as blood clotting. It marks microbes for destruction through three converging routes.

PathwayTriggerInitiator moleculesTypical meaning
AlternativeSpontaneous C3 "tickover" stabilized on a microbial surfaceC3(H₂O), Factor B, Factor D, properdinA surface lacks host complement regulators

All three routes converge on cleavage of C3, the system's hub. C3 is a very abundant blood protein with a hidden reactive group. When C3 is cut, the large fragment, C3b, exposes that group and can stick covalently to nearby surfaces. If that surface is a bacterium, C3b becomes an "eat me" coat. If the surface is a host cell, regulatory proteins usually remove it immediately.

Complement is three tripwires feeding one amplifier

They are not "mines" in the explosive sense. They are surface tests: each pathway asks whether this surface has a microbial pattern, antibody tag, or missing host protection. If yes, it builds the same kind of enzyme on that surface.

Alternative pathway

Always samples surfaces through low-level C3 tickover. Host cells shut it down; microbial surfaces let it amplify.

Lectin pathway

MBL or ficolins bind repeated microbial sugars, then MASP enzymes cut C4 and C2.

Classical pathway

C1q binds antibody Fc or CRP already attached to a surface, then C1r/C1s cut C4 and C2.

Key: a convertase is simply an enzyme that converts one complement component into fragments. C3 convertase cuts C3; C5 convertase cuts C5. The pathway names describe how the surface was first detected, not three separate weapons.

A C3 convertase is therefore the critical machine. The classical and lectin pathways build C4b2a; the alternative pathway builds C3bBb. The names are ugly but the idea is simple: a protein complex stuck on the target surface repeatedly cuts soluble C3 from the blood. Every new C3b molecule that lands nearby can help build more alternative-pathway convertase, so C3 is the system's amplification loop.

"Tickover" means a slow, automatic test — not a tickle In blood, a tiny fraction of C3 spontaneously hydrolyzes all the time. That is called C3 tickover. It creates C3(H2O), which can start the alternative pathway. On host cells, Factor H, Factor I, CD55, and related regulators dismantle the reaction. On a microbial surface with no host regulators, deposited C3b persists, recruits Factor B and Factor D, and turns into a surface C3 convertase. So the alternative pathway is like a continuously armed surface test: "prove you are host, or the coating will grow."

The main products do three different jobs, and they map directly onto the animation above:

PRODUCT 1
C3b — opsonin
C3b binds covalently to the microbial surface as an "eat me" tag. Phagocyte complement receptors CR1 (binds C3b) and CR3 (binds iC3b) grip it, dramatically improving engulfment.
PRODUCT 2
C3a & C5a — anaphylatoxins
Small fragments diffuse away, trigger mast-cell degranulation, raise vascular permeability, and form a chemotactic gradient that pulls neutrophils and monocytes toward the target. C5a is the most potent.
PRODUCT 3
C5b–C9 — MAC
C5b nucleates C6, C7, C8, and many C9 molecules into a ring-shaped pore — the membrane attack complex — that lyses some Gram-negative bacteria directly.

An opsonin is a molecular "eat me" tag that helps phagocytes bind and engulf a target. Complement embodies a recurring immune principle: recognition and killing are separable. A protein tag marks the target; a macrophage or neutrophil executes the cleanup. The terminal pathway adds a direct-kill option, but even there the logic is surface-local: C5b starts the C6-C9 pore only where complement has already been amplified.

4Inflammation is a controlled tissue program

Inflammation is not just swelling. It is a coordinated tissue state that changes blood vessels, endothelial adhesion, local chemistry, and cell recruitment. When PRRs fire, the macrophage's NF-κB program switches on a coordinated set of secreted signals.

macrophage detects microbe (PRR -> NF-kB) | +--> TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6 | -> endothelial activation, fever, acute-phase response | +--> chemokines | -> neutrophils and monocytes follow gradient | +--> prostaglandins and other mediators -> pain, vascular changes

The classic signs are heat, pain, redness, and swelling. Mechanistically, they reflect vasodilation, vascular leak, endothelial activation, leukocyte entry, and nerve sensitization. The local goal is to concentrate plasma proteins and immune cells at the site where they are needed.

The NF-κB cytokine program

NF-κB normally sits inactive in the cytoplasm bound to its inhibitor IκB. PRR signaling activates the IKK kinase, which phosphorylates IκB for degradation; freed NF-κB enters the nucleus and switches on the inflammatory gene set below.

Inflammation becomes dangerous when it spreads systemically or fails to resolve. The same TNF-α that helps contain local infection can contribute to shock when released throughout the body — the core of septic shock.

5Chemokines turn tissue into a map

Chemokines are cytokines specialized for cell movement. They form gradients that tell leukocytes where to go. A neutrophil leaving the bloodstream does not know the pathogen's identity; it follows adhesion signals and chemokine gradients created by infected tissue and resident immune cells.

infected tissue high chemokine concentration ^ | neutrophils crawl from blood toward signal | v blood vessel with activated endothelium

The crawl is a defined sequence — the leukocyte adhesion cascade: rolling (selectins make the neutrophil tumble along the vessel wall) → activation (chemokines signal the cell to firm up) → firm arrest (integrins lock onto endothelial ICAM-1) → transmigration (the cell squeezes between endothelial cells into tissue). This matters because the immune system is spatial. A lymphocyte clone that recognizes an antigen is useless if it never meets the antigen; an antibody is useless if it cannot reach the infected compartment. Chemokines and adhesion molecules give immune responses location.

6Innate signals gate adaptive immunity

Adaptive immunity is powerful enough to damage the host. Therefore a receptor match alone is not always enough. A naive T cell generally needs antigen plus costimulation plus cytokine context. A B cell that binds antigen often needs T-cell help. Dendritic cells become effective activators only after they sense microbial or inflammatory signals.

Antigen without danger context

A self protein or food protein binds a lymphocyte receptor, but no PRR fired and no costimulation appears. Outcome: tolerance, deletion, anergy, or a weak response. The system declines to escalate.

Antigen with innate danger context

PRR signaling matures the dendritic cell — it upregulates the costimulatory molecules B7 (CD80/CD86) and migrates to lymph nodes. Outcome: costimulation, cytokines, lymphocyte expansion.

This is the logic that prevents the immune system from treating every harmless self protein or food protein as an emergency. It also explains why vaccines use adjuvants: an antigen teaches specificity, while an adjuvant supplies the innate danger context (often by triggering PRRs) that licenses a real response. See antigen presentation (chapter 04) and T cells (chapter 06) for the downstream steps.

7What is interferon?

Interferons (IFNs) are cytokines that warn cells about infection, especially viral infection. The name comes from the original observation that a virus-infected cell can release a factor that "interferes" with viral replication in neighboring cells. Interferon is not the kill shot; it is the broadcast that changes nearby cells into a harder place for viruses to replicate.

The type I interferon program induces hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). Some block viral RNA translation, some degrade viral nucleic acids, some slow protein synthesis, and some make antigen presentation more visible by increasing MHC I. That is why the viral timeline starts with infected cells producing interferon before the adaptive response has expanded.

Do not collapse all interferons into one signal. Type I interferon mainly spreads an antiviral state. IFN-γ is more of a macrophage-licensing and cell-mediated-immunity cytokine: NK cells and TH1 cells release it to make macrophages more microbicidal and improve antigen-presentation machinery.
Summary Innate immunity uses inherited receptors, soluble cascades, and tissue-level programs to detect broad danger patterns. PRR families — surface and endosomal TLRs, cytosolic NLRs and the inflammasome, RIG-I-like RNA sensors, and cGAS–STING for cytosolic DNA — each watch a compartment and route through NF-κB or type I interferon. Complement's three pathways converge on a C3 convertase that produces C3b (opsonization), C3a/C5a (inflammation and chemotaxis), and the C5b–C9 membrane attack complex. Cytokines and chemokines recruit help and turn tissue into a map. These signals do more than buy time: they determine whether adaptive B and T cells should be activated at all.
