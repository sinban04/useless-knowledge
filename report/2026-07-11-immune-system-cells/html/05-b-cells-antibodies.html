🏹 B Cells & Antibodies

A B cell is a precision archer that keeps one arrow: a single membrane-bound antibody. When that arrow finally meets its target, the cell copies itself into a factory and fires soluble copies into blood, mucus, and tissue fluid.

Core idea A B cell turns a cell-surface recognition molecule (the BCR) into a soluble molecule (antibody). Antigen does not teach the B cell what to make — the repertoire is built blind by gene rearrangement, and antigen simply selects the rare cell that already matches. With T-cell help, that clone enters a germinal center, mutates and refines its receptor, switches the antibody's effector class, and leaves behind plasma cells and memory.
1What a B cell recognizes

A B cell is an adaptive lymphocyte whose receptor is a membrane-bound antibody. This B-cell receptor (BCR) binds native antigen — proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, toxins, viral surface proteins, bacterial capsules — in its natural three-dimensional shape. That is the first major contrast with T cells, which only ever see short peptides displayed on MHC.

FeatureB cellT cell
ReceptorBCR, a membrane immunoglobulinTCR, a T-cell receptor
Antigen formNative molecular shape (conformational or linear epitope)Peptide bound in an MHC groove
Main effector outputSecreted antibodiesCellular help, killing, regulation
Secreted receptor?Yes — the BCR is released as antibodyNo

When activated, a B cell can become a plasma cell, a dedicated antibody factory. That is the unique contribution of B cells: they convert a single cell-surface recognition molecule into a soluble version that can patrol fluids far from the cell that made it.

Lymphocyte is the family name. B cells and T cells are the two major adaptive lymphocyte classes. NK cells are lymphocyte-lineage cells too, but they use innate-style receptor logic rather than a unique V(D)J-built receptor. Every B cell is a lymphocyte; not every lymphocyte is a B cell.

Anatomy of the antibody / BCR

An immunoglobulin is two identical heavy chains paired with two identical light chains (κ or λ), held in a Y by disulfide bonds. The molecule splits cleanly into function and recognition:

Fab arms — recognition

The two arms each carry a variable (V) region formed by heavy + light pairing. Three hypervariable loops on each chain — the complementarity-determining regions (CDR1/2/3) — form the antigen-binding surface. CDR3 sits at the V(D)J join and is the most diverse, so it dominates specificity.

Fc stem — effector function

The heavy-chain constant (C) region forms the stem. It is the part that complement (C1q), Fc receptors on phagocytes (FcγR) and NK cells (CD16/FcγRIII), and transport machinery (FcRn, pIgR) actually grip. Class switching swaps this stem without touching the Fab.

2How one body makes so many BCRs — V(D)J recombination

Receptor diversity is not encoded gene-by-gene. The genome stores fragments, and developing B cells assemble a unique gene by cutting and pasting DNA. This is V(D)J recombination (Tonegawa, 1983) — the only deliberate, programmed genome editing in normal vertebrate development.

This section gives the B-cell version. The dedicated mechanism page, chapter 12, walks through RAG cutting, junctional diversity, receptor checkpoints, and how germinal centers differ from V(D)J recombination.
SOURCE
Gene-segment libraries
The heavy-chain locus holds many V, several D, and several J segments. Light-chain loci (κ, λ) hold V and J only — no D.
CUT
RAG1/RAG2 cleavage
The RAG1/RAG2 recombinase recognizes recombination signal sequences (RSS) flanking each segment and makes a double-strand break, obeying the 12/23 rule so segments join in the right order.
JOIN
Heavy chain first
D-to-J joins, then V-to-DJ, producing the rearranged VH exon. A successful heavy chain triggers allelic exclusion so only one allele is used.
PAIR
Light chain & assembly
A V-J light-chain rearrangement follows; the VL pairs with VH. Heavy + light pairing multiplies diversity combinatorially.

Three independent multipliers stack to create the repertoire (textbook estimates exceed 1011 possible specificities, far more than the genome could encode directly):

Source of diversityMechanismWhere it acts
CombinatorialWhich V, D, J segments are chosen and joinedHeavy and light loci independently
Heavy/light pairingAny heavy chain can pair with any light chainAt chain assembly
JunctionalExonuclease nibbling at the cut ends, plus P-nucleotides (palindromic) and template-independent N-nucleotides added by TdT (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase)Concentrated at the CDR3 join
Junctional diversity is the crown jewel. Because TdT and exonuclease activity randomly add and remove bases exactly where CDR3 forms, two B cells using identical V, D, and J segments can still bind completely different antigens. This is also why ~2/3 of joins are out of frame and the cell dies — the cost of randomness.

The benefit of building receptors blind is the cost of self-reactivity. B-cell development therefore includes tolerance checkpoints that delete strongly self-reactive cells, force receptor editing (a fresh light-chain rearrangement to escape self-binding), or render them anergic before they mature.

3Clonal selection

Clonal selection (Burnet, 1957) is the operating principle the animation above shows. A naive B cell already carries its receptor before antigen exists. Antigen does not instruct the cell to invent a matching receptor — it selects the rare cell that already matches, and that cell's specificity is inherited by all of its descendants.

large pool of naive B cells (one BCR shape each, built by V(D)J) | v antigen binds the rare matching BCR | v that B-cell clone proliferates (selection, not instruction) | v progeny → plasma cells (antibody) + memory B cells

A matching B cell can therefore expand from rarity to dominance during a single response — exactly the rise-from-the-repertoire the scene animates.

What if no BCR matches? V(D)J recombination gives the body an enormous statistical search library, not a guaranteed catalog of every possible antigen. The system works because the naive B-cell pool is large, BCRs are cross-reactive, pathogens have many possible epitopes, and germinal centers can improve a weak starting match. But a germinal center cannot rescue a clone that never bound antigen at all. If no B cell captures enough antigen, if the useful epitope is hidden, if matching clones were deleted as self-reactive, or if T-cell help is missing, the antibody response can be weak, delayed, or absent.
4T-dependent B-cell activation — the two-signal handshake

Most protein antigens require help from CD4 T cells, especially T follicular helper (Tfh) cells. The B cell is both a recognizer and an antigen-presenting cell in this exchange.

STEP 1
BCR binds
The BCR grips native antigen — signal one.
STEP 2
Internalize
Receptor-mediated endocytosis pulls the antigen inside.
STEP 3
Process & present
The antigen is digested; peptide is loaded onto MHC II and displayed.
STEP 4
Tfh recognizes
A Tfh cell's TCR reads the peptide-MHC II.
STEP 5
License
CD40L-CD40 ligation plus cytokines (IL-21, IL-4) deliver signal two.
STEP 6
Commit
The B cell proliferates, class-switches, hypermutates, and differentiates.
The helper T cell is not carrying antigen from macrophage to B cell. A dendritic cell usually primes the naive CD4 T cell first; a macrophage can later receive TH1 help; a B cell receives Tfh help only after the B cell itself captures antigen through its BCR and presents peptide-MHC II.

This coupling is a safety interlock. The B cell proves it bound antigen; the Tfh cell proves a professional antigen-presenting pathway also raised a response to the same source. The two cells do not need to recognize the exact same epitope, but their epitopes must be physically linked in the same antigenic object. Requiring both sharply reduces the chance that a stray self-reactive B cell receives full activation — the linked-recognition rule.

T-dependent

Protein antigens, Tfh help, germinal centers. Slower start but yields affinity maturation, class switching, and durable memory.

T-independent

Repetitive polysaccharides that strongly cross-link many BCRs at once (e.g. bacterial capsules). Fast and useful against encapsulated bacteria, but weaker memory and little affinity maturation.

5The germinal-center reaction — mutation plus selection

A germinal center (GC) is a transient micro-anatomical structure inside secondary lymphoid tissue (lymph node, spleen, tonsil) where activated B cells refine their antibodies through Darwinian selection on a timescale of days to weeks. It is physically split into two zones that B cells cycle between:

ZoneWhat happensKey players
Dark zoneRapid proliferation; somatic hypermutation rewrites the V-region genes. Cells here are called centroblasts.AID, fast cell cycle
Light zoneSelection. Centrocytes compete to capture limited antigen held on follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) and to win Tfh help. Winners survive and recycle; losers die by apoptosis.FDCs, Tfh, CD40L/IL-21

Somatic hypermutation (SHM) and class-switch recombination (CSR)

Both processes are driven by one enzyme: activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID). AID deaminates cytosine to uracil in single-stranded DNA exposed during transcription; the resulting lesion is processed by error-prone repair (UNG, mismatch repair) into either point mutations or double-strand breaks, depending on where AID acts.

ProcessWhere AID actsWhat changesWhat it accomplishes
Somatic hypermutationV-region exon (variable region)Point mutations at ~106× the background rateCreates BCR variants with different affinities to select among
Class-switch recombinationSwitch (S) regions in the heavy-chain constant locusDeletes one C-region exon set and joins anotherSwaps the Fc effector class while keeping the V-region (specificity)
DARK ZONE centroblasts divide fast → AID mutates V region (SHM) | v LIGHT ZONE centrocytes test new BCR on antigen held by FDC | better binder → captures more antigen → presents more v to Tfh → wins help → survives recycle to dark zone (mutate again) ─OR─ exit as plasma / memory cell x poor binder → no help → apoptosis (selected against)

Affinity maturation is the population-level outcome of repeated dark-zone/light-zone cycling: over successive rounds the average antibody affinity climbs because only the better binders capture enough antigen to earn Tfh survival signals. Class switching, run by the same AID enzyme at a different DNA address, changes what the antibody does after it binds — IgM can become IgG, IgA, or IgE while the antigen-binding surface stays put.

AID is a double-edged enzyme The same controlled mutator that powers antibody quality is dangerous if mistargeted. Off-target AID activity at non-immunoglobulin genes is implicated in the chromosomal translocations behind several B-cell lymphomas — the price of letting a cell deliberately damage its own DNA.
6The five antibody isotypes

Class switching produces five heavy-chain constant regions, each tuned to a different battlefield. The Fab is identical across them after a switch; only the Fc — and therefore the effector partners it recruits — changes.

ClassHeavy chain · formEffector role
IgMμ · secreted pentamerFirst antibody made; ten binding sites give strong agglutination and the most potent classical-pathway complement activation (C1q binding). Low affinity, high avidity.
IgGγ · monomer (4 subclasses)Workhorse of blood and tissue: neutralization, opsonization (binds FcγR on phagocytes), complement fixation, and ADCC. The only isotype actively transported across the placenta (via FcRn) to protect the newborn.
IgAα · monomer in blood, dimer in secretionsDominant antibody of mucosal surfaces. Secretory IgA is transported across epithelium by the polymeric Ig receptor (pIgR) into gut, airway, tears, and breast milk, where it blocks pathogen attachment.
IgEε · monomerBinds FcεRI on mast cells and basophils. Cross-linking triggers degranulation — central to parasite (helminth) defense and to allergy/anaphylaxis.
IgDδ · monomerCo-expressed with IgM as the BCR on mature naive B cells; little-secreted, its systemic role is still partly unsettled.
7What antibodies do — six effector mechanisms

An antibody is not only a binding molecule; it is a bridge between recognition and effector machinery. The Fab decides what to grab; the Fc decides what happens next. The same separable design seen with complement and Fc receptors recurs here: tagging and action are independent.

FunctionMechanism (which part of the antibody acts)Result
NeutralizationFab arms physically block a toxin's active site or a virus's receptor-binding siteToxin inert; virus cannot enter cells
OpsonizationFc region (IgG) binds FcγR on macrophages/neutrophilsPhagocyte engulfs the coated microbe
Complement activationClustered Fc (IgM/IgG) recruits C1q — the classical pathwayC3b deposition, MAC formation, inflammation
Agglutination / precipitationMultivalent binding cross-links many particles or soluble moleculesClumps are easier to clear and neutralize
ADCCAn NK cell's CD16 (FcγRIII) binds antibody Fc on a coated targetNK cell releases perforin/granzyme and kills the target
Mucosal protectionSecretory IgA binds pathogens within the mucus layerReduced attachment and entry at the epithelial surface
One target, many fates. A single antibody-coated bacterium can be neutralized, opsonized for a macrophage, lysed by complement, and clumped for clearance simultaneously — because the constant region speaks to several effector systems at once. Antibody-dependent killing by NK cells is detailed in chapter 08.
8How antibodies and neutrophils work together

A neutrophil is a short-lived, fast-arriving phagocyte specialized for acute bacterial and fungal inflammation. Under the microscope it has a segmented, multi-lobed nucleus and many granules packed with antimicrobial enzymes. That lobed nucleus is why neutrophils are often called polymorphonuclear leukocytes or PMNs.

Antibody does not usually kill a bacterium by itself. It turns the bacterium into a better target for neutrophils and macrophages. The Fab arms bind the microbial antigen; the Fc stems point outward as handles for immune cells.

Macrophage versus neutrophil. Both can phagocytose opsonized microbes. Neutrophils arrive fast from blood, kill aggressively, and often die at the site. Macrophages live longer in tissue, coordinate cytokines and repair, and present antigen more meaningfully.
9What dendritic cells look like and do

A dendritic cell is the professional scout of adaptive immunity. It has a branched, star-like shape with long membrane projections that probe tissue and make contacts with T cells. Its job is not to secrete antibody; its job is to carry antigen plus danger context from tissue to the lymph node.

TISSUE
Sample
Immature dendritic cells take up antigen by macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, and receptor-mediated capture.
DANGER

PRR signals raise MHC, B7/CD80/CD86, and CCR7; the cell stops being mainly a sampler and becomes an instructor.
LYMPHATIC

LYMPH NODE
Prime

The clean division A B cell is the antibody specialist. A dendritic cell is the naive-T-cell activation specialist. The B cell can present antigen too, but mainly after its BCR captures a matching antigen; the dendritic cell is built to start the T-cell response in the first place.
10Plasma cells and memory B cells

After activation and germinal-center selection, B-cell progeny diverge into output cells. This is the durable part of the response — the part a vaccine is trying to leave behind.

FateFunction
Short-lived plasma cellRapid antibody production during the acute response; dies within days
Long-lived plasma cellPersistent antibody secretion for years, usually from bone-marrow survival niches
Memory B cellQuiescent cell poised for a fast, high-quality recall response on re-exposure

Memory B cells are not leftovers. Many have already passed through class switching and affinity maturation, so on re-exposure they reactivate faster than naive cells and start from a higher-quality antibody. The kinetics of that secondary response are the subject of chapter 09, and how memory persists (and how tolerance keeps it from turning on self) is covered in chapter 10.

Summary B cells solve the extracellular specificity problem. Each carries a unique BCR built blind by V(D)J recombination — combinatorial choice, heavy/light pairing, and RAG-cut, TdT-driven junctional diversity at CDR3. Antigen selects the rare matching clone. With a CD40L/cytokine handshake from a Tfh cell, that clone enters a germinal center, where AID powers both somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination. Antibody then bridges recognition to effector cells: IgG Fc and complement tags let neutrophils and macrophages phagocytose targets, while dendritic cells handle the separate job of starting naive T-cell responses. The durable output is long-lived plasma cells and memory B cells.
